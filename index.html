Teaching philosophy

Four things guide how I run my classroom every day. Here is what each one looks like in practice.

Lifelong learning starts with earned satisfaction

Accomplishment feels good, and that feeling is what keeps someone learning long after the class ends. I make progress visible so students actually see it happen.

Example: Student work goes up along the back wall of my classroom, early drafts next to finished pieces. In one writing unit, students revised an essay through several drafts, and the strongest ones ran in the school newspaper.

From a student: a second grader wrote me a thank-you note last year. Her name is covered here since she's a minor.

Emotional needs come first

A student who feels anxious, unsafe, or overlooked is not ready to learn. Feeling secure is the necessary foundation. Students bring different needs into the room, from ADHD to autism to plain nerves, and nearly all of them settle when the routine and the organization stay the same.

Example: Clear expectations, consistent routines, and predictable transitions. For a few students who need more structure, that predictability is the difference between staying on task and losing the thread.

Students are accountable for their actions

High expectations are a form of respect. When a choice causes a problem, we talk about the choice and what happens next, calmly and directly.

Example: In my team point system, points are earned for team behavior and each team decides how to spend them, so peers hold one another to the standard. When a team falls short, we talk about what didn't work and try again next round.

Treat every child the way I want mine treated

I am a father of two, and I hold myself to one standard: treat each student the way I would want a teacher to treat my own kids. That means patience, kindness, and respect, and it means listening when a student has something to say.

Example: At my Grade 2 open class, I told parents this directly. It is also why I take a question seriously even mid-lesson, even when it slows things down.

Classroom management

I built a team-based positive reinforcement system grounded in Kagan cooperative learning research. Points are awarded for team behavior, not individual behavior, and each team decides together how to spend what it has earned.

That makes it different from individual token systems such as ClassDojo. Students have to hold one another accountable, and the team stays cohesive because everyone shares the outcome.

  • Casual but strict, built through consistent routines rather than rigid control
  • 24 forty-minute periods a week, with classes rotating through my room all day
  • New at Dongsung in 2026. My observation is that behavior has improved. I have not yet tracked referral or transition-time data

Ribbon Overlay, version 3

I couldn't find a tool that did everything I needed for classroom management, so I built one myself, with a little help from Claude. This is Ribbon, version 3.

It's a lightweight tool that starts as a small floating button sitting on top of anything on your screen: a PowerPoint, a website, an ebook, a PDF, whatever you're teaching from. Click it, and it expands into a full ribbon of tools, still floating on top of everything underneath. I've since combined it with a separate whiteboard website, which replicates most of what my standalone lesson decks do, with far less prep time.

Built in

  • A countdown timer
  • Group and team points
  • A group-order randomizer
  • A random student selector tied to Kagan cooperative learning roles — each desk is labeled with a color for its group and a letter (A/B/C/D) for its role within that group, so the ribbon can call on "Blue C" and every student knows exactly who that is
  • A customizable celebration pop-up. One class is obsessed with moon shoes, the 90s toy, long story, so I loaded a picture of one in and click it whenever a student does something above and beyond, on top of the usual team point
  • An attendance module that tracks daily attendance and behavior strikes over the long term

Vocabulary Assessment System: how the words get chosen

An 800-word vocabulary curriculum across five grades and four proficiency tiers. Every word passes through six weighted gates, anchored to each grade-and-level cell's own prior-semester profile rather than one flat difficulty cutoff, so a high-level cell still demands rarer words than a low-level one even after the whole list moves up a notch.

The six gates

  • Corpus frequency (~35%) — cell-anchored to each cell's own prior-semester 10th percentile, not a flat threshold
  • Lexical utility / CEFR tier (~20%) — weights academic-utility words
  • Age-of-acquisition (~15%) — added after raw frequency let in age-inappropriate abstract words
  • Morphological generativity (~15%) — favors words that seed a family (base to derivative)
  • Imageability/concreteness (~10%) — same reasoning as age-of-acquisition, protects against adult-corpus bias
  • Curriculum alignment and spelling variety (~5%) — a tie-breaker, not a gate

Hard rules on top of the weighting

  • No word from the prior semester's list can reappear
  • No word repeats within the same proficiency level across grades
  • Only base-form headwords, even where a sentence uses an inflected form
  • Part-of-speech mix is targeted by level: harder levels skew noun and verb heavy, easier levels allow more adjectives
  • At Grades 2 and 3 only, a word can drop below the frequency floor if it's highly concrete and acquired early, since adult corpora undercount ordinary children's words like apple or frog

Concreteness and age-of-acquisition weren't in the first version. I tested raw adult-corpus frequency alone first, and it let rare adult-domain words through while blocking perfectly good concrete words a child already knows. Adding those two gates fixed it.

I check my own work: every grade-and-level cell's average word length moved up slightly from last semester, never backward, never by more than about half a letter. That was the design goal — extend gradually, not jump — and the numbers confirm it held. Paired with an AI-assisted process that generates print-ready tests and answer keys directly from the word lists.
